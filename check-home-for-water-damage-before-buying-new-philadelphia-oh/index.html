How to Check a Home for Water Damage Before Buying in New Philadelphia, OH

Buyers rarely see water damage in a showing, but they can learn to spot the signs. Here is what to look at, what to ask and when to call in help.

By the New Philadelphia Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 9, 2026.

(740) 231-9132
Call Now

Water damage is one of the costliest surprises in a home purchase. It can hide behind fresh paint, under new flooring or inside a wall. A careful walk through and a good inspection can find most of it.

This article is general information. A licensed home inspector is the right person to inspect a house, and an attorney or agent can advise on contracts.

Start outside

Walk the property before you go in. Look at the grading near the foundation, since soil should slope away from the house. Check gutters and downspouts, and look for downspouts that dump next to the foundation.

Look at the roof for missing or curled shingles and at the foundation for cracks, stains or white mineral deposits. Look for window wells filled with debris and for signs that water has ponded near the house.

Trust your nose

A musty smell is one of the best clues. A seller who overuses air fresheners or candles may be trying to hide it. Smell closets, basements and cabinets, and if you smell something, ask why. Moisture can sit inside walls with no stain at all, which our wall and drywall water damage repair page explains.

Fresh paint and new flooring

New paint on one patch of ceiling, new flooring in only the basement or a freshly finished lower wall can be innocent. They can also cover a past problem. Ask what was done and when, and request receipts.

Ask the seller questions

  • Has there ever been a leak or flood, and when?
  • Who repaired it and do you have records?
  • Was an insurance claim made?
  • Does the basement or crawl space ever get damp?
  • Does the sump pump run, and when was it last replaced?
  • How old are the roof and the water heater?

The inspection

A home inspector uses a moisture meter and a thermal camera in many cases and will note signs of water. If the report flags moisture, ask for a follow up with a specialist. We can read moisture and describe what we find. We do not replace a home inspector.

Ask your inspector about the sump pump, the grading, the crawl space and the attic. These are common water entry points. Our crawl space water damage and basement water damage cleanup pages describe the kinds of damage involved.

Flood and insurance checks

Look up the address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. Ask your insurance agent for quotes before you close, including flood and sewer backup coverage. A flood zone address often means a required flood policy if you have a mortgage.

When to bring in a specialist

A home inspection is a broad look at a house. If the inspector notes high moisture, stains or smells, bring in someone who can measure more closely. A moisture survey with meters and a thermal camera can map the wet areas and show whether the water is old or active.

Ask the specialist to put findings in writing, with photos and readings. Then ask for a scope and price of the repair work, so you can compare the number with the asking price.

If the damage is recent and the cause is unfixed, think carefully. A known, repaired and dry problem is a manageable risk. An active leak in a house you do not yet own is a decision to pause on.

Red flags worth a second look

Some combinations deserve extra attention. Fresh paint on a basement wall paired with a dehumidifier running full time suggests the owners are fighting damp air. A sump pump with no discharge pipe, or one that discharges next to the foundation, suggests water is being recycled back into the soil.

Mismatched flooring in one room, a new section of drywall in a ceiling and a closed-off crawl space hatch are also worth asking about. None of these prove a problem, but each deserves a clear answer and a record.

According to the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, moisture readings against dry standards are how a structure is judged dry, which is why buyers should ask for them.

Buying near the Tuscarawas River

New Philadelphia and Dover sit along the Tuscarawas River, which has flooded before. The March 1913 flood hit Dover and the county hard. Parcels near the river or in low areas deserve a closer look at the FEMA map and at the basement.

Ohio sellers complete a property disclosure form, which includes questions about water problems. Read it carefully and compare it with what you see.

Buyer FAQ

Can a home inspector find all water damage?

Not always. Inspectors see what is visible and measurable. A specialist can read moisture where more is needed.

Is a past leak a deal breaker?

Not always. A documented repair with dry readings may be fine. An unexplained or ongoing leak is more concerning.
