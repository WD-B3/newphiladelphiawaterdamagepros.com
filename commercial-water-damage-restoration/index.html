When a commercial water loss is an emergency

Water in a commercial building is an emergency when it is near electrical equipment, inventory, a server closet or a floor shared with other tenants. Water also travels. A leak in a second floor suite can reach the unit below within hours, which turns one claim into several.

Call as soon as you see water, even when the source is unknown. Tell the dispatcher the building type, whether it is a single tenant space or multi-unit, and who has authority to approve work. After hours, a property manager or owner may need to be on the line.

Power and fire systems

Water near panels, lighting or a sprinkler riser is a safety problem first. Do not touch wet electrical equipment. Call the electric utility or 911 for any electrical or gas risk before you call our line.

Where commercial water losses start

Commercial buildings fail differently from houses. Flat roofs pond water and leak at seams. Rooftop HVAC units drain into ceilings.

Restaurants lose grease trap and floor drain lines. Buildings around the Tuscarawas River corridor and the busy US 250 and OH 39 stretches can also take water from storms and rising runoff.

Drop ceilings hide the evidence. Stained tiles, sagging grids and a musty smell are signs that water has been running longer than you think. Our ceiling water damage repair page covers the ceiling side of the problem.

How we work on a business

Commercial jobs need a plan that protects operations as well as materials.

Walk the loss

Find the source, map the wet areas and note who is affected.

Protect what matters

Lift or relocate inventory, protect records and isolate wet zones from dry ones.

Extract and dry

Remove standing water, then run air movers and dehumidifiers with daily moisture readings.

Document everything

Photos, moisture logs and room-by-room notes for your insurer and landlord.

Phased work is common. Priority areas such as a sales floor or a kitchen can be dried first so part of the business can reopen while other rooms finish. Drying methods are explained on our structural drying page.

Single tenant versus multi-unit

Single tenant space

  • You control the source and the access.
  • One owner or manager approves the work.
  • Downtime affects one business.

Multi-unit or shared building

  • Water often crosses tenant lines.
  • Landlord, tenants and insurers may all be involved.
  • Documentation protects every party.

In a multi-unit building, a pipe failure upstairs may be the landlord's loss while the stock on the floor is the tenant's. Keep written notes of who approved what and when.

Commercial water damage FAQ

Do you respond to business water losses outside office hours?

Yes. Commercial losses are taken any hour, weekends included, and we ask early about tenants, power and access.

Can part of my business reopen while drying continues?

Often yes. Drying can be phased so priority areas are finished first, depending on the water category and what is still wet.

Who should be on the call, the owner or the property manager?

Whoever can approve work and give building access. In shared buildings, bring the landlord or manager in early.

Do you document the loss for the insurance claim?

Yes. We provide photos, moisture readings and room-by-room notes you can hand to your insurer. We do not promise coverage.

Do you work on apartment buildings and multi-unit properties?

Yes. Multi-unit losses need coordination between units, so we keep notes of which areas are affected and who approved the work.
