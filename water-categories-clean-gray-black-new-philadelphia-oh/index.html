Clean, Gray and Black Water: What the Categories Mean in New Philadelphia, OH

Water damage is sorted into three categories. The category decides what can be dried, what must be thrown away and how careful the crew has to be.

Call Now

Not all water damage is the same. Water that comes from a clean supply line is very different from water that comes from a toilet or a river. The industry sorts water into three categories, and the category shapes every decision that follows.

The categories come from the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, the industry standard for water damage restoration. This article explains them in plain language.

Category 1: clean water

Category 1 water comes from a sanitary source, such as a broken supply line, a faucet or a tub overflow with no contamination. It is clean when it escapes, but it does not stay that way. Materials, dust and time can degrade it.

Clean water can often be extracted and the structure dried, with carpet, pad and drywall saved in many cases if the response is quick. The longer it sits, the more it degrades into the next category.

Category 2: gray water

Category 2 water has some contamination that could cause discomfort. Typical sources include dishwasher and washing machine discharge, a toilet overflow with urine but no feces and some sump pump overflows. It is not sewage, but it is not clean.

Cleanup is more cautious. Porous materials like carpet pad are often removed, and hard surfaces are cleaned and sanitized. Gray water left for a day or two can turn into black water.

Category 3: black water

Category 3 water is grossly contaminated. Sewage backing up, flood water from a river and water that has been standing and has bacteria growth are Category 3. It can carry bacteria, viruses and other harmful material.

Black water means porous materials that touched it, such as carpet, pad, insulation and drywall, are usually removed and discarded. Hard surfaces are cleaned and disinfected. This work needs protective gear and containment, and it is not a do-it-yourself job.

1 CleanSupply line, faucet, tub overflowDry and save most materials.
2 GrayDishwasher, washer, some sump overflowRemove porous items, clean and dry.
3 BlackSewage, river flood water, long-standing waterRemove porous items, sanitize, dry.

Water can change category

Water moves down in category over time and as it touches things. A clean supply line leak that runs into a dirty crawl space can become gray. Clean water that sits for days can become gray or black. That is why timing matters so much.

The temperature of the building, the materials the water touches and how long it sits all change the result. A crew assesses on arrival and may reclassify the water as the job goes on.

Why the category matters to you

The category drives safety, cost and insurance. A Category 3 loss costs more because more material is removed and the work is more controlled. Your insurer may also treat the cause differently depending on the source.

Knowing the category helps you decide what to do while you wait. For clean water, you can usually move furniture and lift rugs. For gray and black water, keep people and pets away, and avoid touching it.

What homeowners can do

Identify the source

Where did the water come from, and was it clean when it started?

Keep people out of gray and black water

Wear boots and gloves if you must go in, and avoid contact.

Do not use a household vacuum

It is a shock hazard and it spreads contamination.

Call and describe it

Tell the dispatcher the source and how long the water has been there.

How crews test and classify water

A technician looks at the source first, then at how long the water has been present and what it touched. Clean supply water that ran for an hour across a tile floor is a different situation from the same water that sat for three days in a carpeted basement.

Crews also look for signs of contamination, like odor, discoloration, debris and microbial growth. Moisture meters tell how far the water has gone, but the category decision comes from the source and the history. When there is doubt, crews classify toward the more cautious category.

You can help by telling the crew everything you know: when the leak started, where it came from and whether anyone cleaned up already. That history changes the plan.

Floodwater in the Tuscarawas Valley

Water from river flooding is treated as Category 3 because it carries soil, runoff and contaminants. Tuscarawas County has a history of this kind of flooding, including the March 1913 event that hit Dover and the surrounding area, and that event led to a district wide flood control system run by the Muskingum Watershed Conservancy District.

If river water enters a basement, treat it as contaminated until a professional says otherwise.

Water categories FAQ

Can I clean up Category 1 water myself?

A small clean spill, yes. A soaked room needs extraction and drying with equipment, and deeper water needs a professional.

How fast does clean water become gray?

It depends on the materials and the building. Treat 24 to 48 hours as the window where it can degrade.

Is rain water Category 1?

Not once it enters a building through a roof or wall. It picks up dust and debris and is often treated as Category 2.
